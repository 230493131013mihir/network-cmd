Threat detection fundamentals

20 min read Advanced No setup required

Detection vs prevention

Security teams often talk about prevention — firewalls, patching, access controls — as the primary goal. But no prevention is perfect. Threat detection is the discipline that answers: when prevention fails, how quickly do we know?

The industry benchmark is mean time to detect (MTTD) and mean time to respond (MTTR). In 2023, the median time for organizations to detect a breach was around 16 days. With good detection engineering, that can drop to minutes.

What are we looking for?

Threats leave traces. These traces fall into two broad categories:

Indicators of Compromise (IoCs)

IoCs are specific artifacts that indicate a system has been compromised. They include:

  • Known malicious IP addresses or domains
  • File hashes matching known malware
  • Specific registry keys created by malware
  • Unusual process names or paths

IoCs are the easiest to detect but also the easiest for attackers to change. A threat actor can rotate their C2 IP in minutes.

Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs)

TTPs describe how attackers operate — the patterns of behavior that make up an attack. These are much harder to change. Even if an attacker switches their C2 IP, they still need to establish persistence, move laterally, and exfiltrate data — and those behaviors follow predictable patterns.

The MITRE ATT&CK framework (covered in lesson 5) is the industry-standard catalog of TTPs used by real threat actors.

KEY IDEA

Detecting TTPs rather than IoCs is harder to build but far more durable. An attacker can change their tools overnight. They can't easily change how they think about achieving their objectives.

The detection pipeline

Raw logs are unusable at scale. A detection pipeline transforms them into actionable alerts:

DETECTION PIPELINE
1. Collection    Log sources → log shipper (Filebeat, Fluentd)
                          ↓
2. Ingestion     SIEM or data lake (Splunk, Elastic, Chronicle)
                          ↓
3. Normalization Parse and enrich raw logs → structured events
                          ↓
4. Detection     Rules run against events → alerts
                          ↓
5. Triage        Analyst reviews alerts → true/false positive
                          ↓
6. Response      Contain, investigate, remediate

Signal vs noise

The biggest practical challenge in threat detection isn't writing rules — it's keeping the false positive rate low enough that analysts don't develop "alert fatigue" and start ignoring everything.

A well-tuned detection should have a signal-to-noise ratio where the vast majority of alerts are genuine. Getting there requires:

  • Starting with high-confidence, low-volume detections
  • Baselining normal behavior before alerting on deviations
  • Adding context (asset criticality, user role) to prioritize
  • Continuous tuning based on analyst feedback
ANTI-PATTERN

Don't try to detect everything at once. A team drowning in 10,000 alerts a day that they can't triage is worse than a team with 20 high-quality alerts. Quality over quantity, always.

Your first three detections

If you're starting from scratch, here are three high-confidence, high-value detections to implement first:

  1. Failed login bursts — more than 10 failed logins for a single account in 5 minutes. Catches password spraying and brute force.
  2. New admin account creation — any new user added to an admin group outside of your change window. Privilege escalation is a critical step in most attacks.
  3. Outbound connection to newly-registered domain — domains registered within the last 30 days are a common C2 indicator. Most legitimate services use established domains.
