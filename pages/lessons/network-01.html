What is network monitoring?

18 min read Beginner No setup required

Why monitoring matters

Every device on a network produces a constant stream of traffic. When things are working well, that stream follows predictable patterns — a spike at 9am as people start work, a lull at lunch, another peak mid-afternoon. Network monitoring is the practice of watching that stream and knowing when something breaks the pattern.

Without monitoring, you find out about problems from users complaining, or from customers who can't reach your service, or — worst — from a breach report weeks after the fact. With monitoring, you find out from an alert before the impact spreads.

KEY IDEA

Monitoring isn't just about detecting attacks. Most alerts you'll ever respond to are misconfigurations, hardware failures, or capacity issues — not adversaries. A good monitoring setup catches all of them.

The three layers of network monitoring

Network monitoring operates at three different levels of detail, each answering different questions:

1. Flow monitoring

Flow data tells you who talked to whom, for how long, and how much data moved. It doesn't contain the contents of conversations — just the metadata. Think of it like a phone bill: you see the numbers dialed and call duration, but not what was said.

Flow protocols include NetFlow (Cisco), IPFIX (the open standard), and sFlow. Most enterprise routers and switches can export this data to a collector.

2. Packet capture

Packet capture (PCAP) gives you the full contents of every packet on the wire. This is the most detailed view — you can reconstruct entire conversations, extract files, and see exactly what was transmitted. The trade-off is volume: a 1Gbps link generates roughly 450GB of PCAPs per hour.

Tools: Wireshark (GUI), tcpdump (CLI), tshark (CLI with Wireshark's dissectors).

3. Device metrics

CPU load, interface error rates, memory usage, BGP peer states — these come from the devices themselves via SNMP, NETCONF, or streaming telemetry. They tell you about the health of your infrastructure rather than the traffic flowing through it.

What does "normal" look like?

You can't detect abnormal without first understanding normal. For a typical corporate network, normal might include:

  • DNS queries going to your internal resolvers (e.g. 10.0.0.1), not to external IPs
  • Web traffic on ports 80 and 443 through a proxy
  • Workstation-to-server traffic, but not workstation-to-workstation (lateral movement is a red flag)
  • Mail flow in and out of your MX servers
  • Backup jobs running between midnight and 4am

Anything that deviates from this baseline deserves a look. A workstation querying a DNS server in another country at 3am is a textbook indicator of a C2 (command-and-control) beacon.

COMMON MISTAKE

Don't try to alert on everything unusual from day one. You'll drown in noise. Start by baselining for two weeks, then write your first three or four high-confidence alerts before expanding.

A simple monitoring stack

Here's a minimal stack that covers all three layers for a small network:

STACK OVERVIEW 
# Flow collection
Router → exports NetFlow → ntopng or Elastiflow

# Packet capture (on a mirror/SPAN port)
Network tap → tcpdump → PCAP storage
            → Zeek    → structured logs → SIEM

# Device metrics
Devices → SNMP poll → Prometheus + SNMP Exporter
        → Grafana for dashboards

In later lessons we'll set up each of these components. For now, the important thing is understanding what each layer is for — and that you don't need all three from day one.

Quick wins to implement today

If you manage a network right now and want to start immediately, here are three things you can do without any new tooling:

  1. Enable logging on your firewall. Even if you're not collecting centrally yet, firewall logs are the most valuable single source of truth about what's entering and leaving your network.
  2. Check your DNS logs. Most routers and firewalls log DNS queries. Look for queries to newly-registered domains or domains with high entropy in the name (a sign of domain-generation algorithms).
  3. Run netstat -an on your servers. Check for unexpected listening ports or connections to unfamiliar external IPs. Takes two minutes and has caught real intrusions.
